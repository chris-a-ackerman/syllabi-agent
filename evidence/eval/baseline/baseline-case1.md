## Class focus
**Topic:** Technology and job quality: monitoring and surveillance

The core question for this class is how workplace monitoring technologies change job quality. Canvas frames the issue as a tradeoff: these tools may improve efficiency, productivity, safety, and reward effort, but they can also reduce worker autonomy, demotivate people, dehumanize work, and fail to measure what actually matters.

The assigned materials center on three pieces:

From the Canvas prompt alone, the throughline is:
- monitoring systems are spreading quickly;

- their real effects may be uneven across firms and workers;
- trucking is a concrete industry case where the promises and costs of surveillance are easier to see.

## Likely class questions
Canvas explicitly asks you to be ready for these:

## A useful way to think about the discussion
Come in with a structured view rather than a blanket pro/anti-surveillance position.

### 1. Separate outcomes from mechanisms
Ask:
- Is the technology measuring something that genuinely matters?
- Is it measuring outputs, behavior proxies, or compliance theater?
- Does it help workers do better work, or mainly make them easier to control?

That distinction will help you compare “safety-enhancing” monitoring with more extractive or punitive systems.

### 2. Track distributional effects
A strong class contribution here is to ask not just “does it work?” but:
- Who captures the productivity gains?
- Who loses autonomy, privacy, discretion, or bargaining power?
- Are workers able to contest bad metrics or false signals?
- Do managers become more informed, or just more dependent on crude dashboards?

### 3. Distinguish high-stakes from low-stakes monitoring
You’ll probably be on solid ground if you distinguish among:
- **Safety monitoring** (fatigue, dangerous driving, accidents)
- **Performance monitoring** (speed, output, idle time)
- **Behavioral surveillance** (location, keystrokes, cameras, biometrics)

A defensible position is that some monitoring may be justified when the safety case is strong and the data are tightly tied to real risks, but much harder to justify when the system mainly intensifies work or substitutes opaque metrics for judgment.

## Talking points you can use
- Monitoring can create value when it improves safety or coordination, but it becomes problematic when it treats workers as optimization targets rather than skilled decision-makers.
- Efficiency gains are not enough on their own; the class prompt pushes toward asking whether the gains are achieved in ways consistent with dignity, autonomy, and fairness.
- Trucking is a good stress test because surveillance may claim to reduce accidents, but it can also shift power toward firms, insurers, and platform managers while narrowing worker discretion.
- A key policy question is not only whether monitoring should exist, but what governance should surround it: transparency, worker consent, appeal rights, limits on use, and restrictions on punitive or manipulative applications.

## A draft position you could bring to class
A balanced view:

> Some workplace monitoring is defensible when it is narrowly tailored to real safety or quality goals, transparent to workers, and paired with due process. But monitoring becomes harmful when firms use proxy metrics to intensify work, reduce autonomy, or offload risk onto workers. The main question is not whether monitoring increases efficiency, but whether the gains justify the losses in dignity, privacy, and worker control — and whether those gains are shared.

## If you speak in class
Three concise points that would likely play well:
1. **Not all monitoring is the same** — safety monitoring and productivity scoring should not be evaluated by the same standard.
2. **Metrics shape behavior** — once firms optimize around measurable proxies, they may distort the work itself.
3. **Governance matters** — transparency, contestability, and worker voice may matter as much as the technology.
